Fear is more specific than anxiety.
Anxiety loops without an object.
Fear has one — the appointment on Thursday, the person you have to see, the phone call at eleven at night, the dark hallway.

That difference matters, because a verse aimed at a vague dread does not land on a very particular one.
So these ten are grouped by what they are afraid of, and each group ends with the peace that answers it.

If what you are carrying is the looping kind rather than the specific kind, there is a separate guide for
[verses for anxiety](/blog/what-bible-verses-help-when-you-feel-anxious) that stays out of this one's way.

## Fear of people

Three passages for the fear that has a face.

### Psalm 27:1

> The LORD is my light and my salvation; whom shall I fear? the LORD is the strength of my life; of whom shall I be afraid?

The psalm asks the question twice, and the second time it narrows: not *what*, but *whom*.
It is a verse written by someone who had enemies with names.

### Psalm 56:4

> In God I will praise his word, in God I have put my trust; I will not fear what flesh can do unto me.

"Flesh" is the operative word.
It puts a limit on the threat — this person is flesh, and so are you, and neither of you is the final authority in the room.

### Psalm 118:6

> The LORD is on my side; I will not fear: what can man do unto me?

Almost the same sentence as the last one, said as a question rather than a statement.
Sometimes the question is the prayer.

## Fear at night

Four passages that assume you are lying down and cannot sleep.

### Psalm 4:8

> I will both lay me down in peace, and sleep: for thou, LORD, only makest me dwell in safety.

Note the order.
Peace, then sleep — and the reason given is not that the situation was resolved.
It is that someone else is keeping watch.

### Psalm 3:5

> I laid me down and slept; I awaked; for the LORD sustained me.

This is a verse written on the morning after.
"I awaked" is the whole testimony — the night happened, the fear was real, and here you are.

### Proverbs 3:24

> When thou liest down, thou shalt not be afraid: yea, thou shalt lie down, and thy sleep shall be sweet.

"Sweet" is an odd word for a proverb about fear, and it is deliberate.
The promise is not merely that you will stop being afraid, but that rest becomes good again.

### Psalm 91:5

> Thou shalt not be afraid for the terror by night; nor for the arrow that flieth by day;

The psalm names the night terror first, which is where most fear actually lives.
Whatever is in the dark is still inside the psalm.

## Fear of what is coming

Three for the fear that arrives by notification.

### Psalm 46:2

> Therefore will not we fear, though the earth be removed, and though the mountains be carried into the midst of the sea;

The scenario is as large as it can get — the ground itself gone.
The response is still "will not we fear," and the psalm's reason comes a few verses later: God is in the midst of her, she shall not be moved.

### Psalm 112:7

> He shall not be afraid of evil tidings: his heart is fixed, trusting in the LORD.

This is the most practical verse in the list for anyone who reads the news at night.
"Evil tidings" assumes bad news arrives.
The difference is a fixed heart, not a quiet inbox.

### 2 Timothy 1:7

> For God hath not given us the spirit of fear; but of power, and of love, and of a sound mind.

Worth reading slowly, because it reframes fear as something handed to you rather than something you produced.
If it was given, it can be put down.

## Peace verses that answer fear

Four passages where the Bible stops describing the fear and describes the alternative.

### Isaiah 26:3

> Thou wilt keep him in perfect peace, whose mind is stayed on thee: because he trusteth in thee.

"Stayed" is an old word for propped against something.
The peace is not the reward for a calm mind; it is what a mind propped on God is kept in.

### John 14:27

> Peace I leave with you, my peace I give unto you: not as the world giveth, give I unto you. Let not your heart be troubled, neither let it be afraid.

Jesus says this hours before his arrest.
That timing is the argument — the peace he is describing was not produced by things going well.

### Philippians 4:7

> And the peace of God, which passeth all understanding, shall keep your hearts and minds through Christ Jesus.

"Keep" here is a garrison word — soldiers stationed at a gate.
It does not promise that the fear never approaches, only that it does not get to walk in unguarded.

### 2 Thessalonians 3:16

> Now the Lord of peace himself give you peace always by all means. The Lord be with you all.

"By all means" is the widest phrase in the list.
There is no situation excluded from it.

## What to do with these at eleven at night

A short method, because a list of ten verses does not help at eleven at night.

1. **Name the fear in one sentence.** "I am afraid of Thursday" is enough. Fear that stays vague stays large.
2. **Pick one verse from the matching group.** One, not ten. Read it twice, slowly.
3. **Say the reason out loud.** Every verse above gives a reason, and the reason is always a person, not an outcome.
4. **Leave the outcome alone.** None of these verses promise the appointment goes well. They promise you are not alone in it.

That last point is where most people stop reading, and it is also the only honest thing a verse can offer at 2am.

## Which verse for which fear

- **A person you have to face:** Psalm 27:1 — whom shall I fear.
- **What they could do to you:** Psalm 56:4 — they are flesh, and so are you.
- **Cannot sleep:** Psalm 4:8 — peace first, then sleep.
- **Just got bad news:** Psalm 112:7 — an evil tiding is assumed, a fixed heart is promised.
- **Dread with no clear shape:** 2 Timothy 1:7 — fear was given, so it can be put down.
- **You want the peace, not the analysis:** Isaiah 26:3 — a mind propped somewhere.
