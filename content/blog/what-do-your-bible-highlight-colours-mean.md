Almost everyone starts with one highlighter.

Whatever the first colour in the row happens to be. It feels right for a while, because everything you mark really is something you found important.

Then you come back to the same chapter a year later, and every verse on the page is the same colour. The highlighting is still there. It just stopped telling you anything.

Colour is a way of recording *what kind* of thing you found, not only that you found it. That is the whole idea behind a small colour system, and it takes about two minutes to set up once.

## Why one colour runs out of meaning

A single colour answers one question: "was this worth noting?" After a few months of reading, the answer is almost always yes, and the marks stop discriminating.

A handful of colours answers a more useful question: *why* did this stop me?

- Is this a verse about who Christ is and what he did?
- Is it a verse about God's character?
- Is it instruction for how to live?
- Is it a promise to hold onto?
- Is it a warning I would rather skim past?

Those are five different things, and they belong in five different colours.

## The five colours and what goes in each

This is the system the KJV app ships with. Tap a verse, choose Highlight, and the five colours arrive with suggested themes already attached.

### Red · Christ

Who he is, what he did, what he said he would do. Isaiah 53 is the obvious ground:

> But he was wounded for our transgressions, he was bruised for our iniquities: the chastisement of our peace was upon him; and with his stripes we are healed.
> — Isaiah 53:5

Red here marks atonement and salvation, not general importance. If everything urgent goes red, the cross stops standing out.

One thing worth knowing: the words of Christ are already red in the Gospels by default, but that is a different layer — red letters colour the *text*, a highlight sits *behind* it. The two never fight each other, and you can change the red-letter shade or switch the feature off in Settings → Widgets → App Widget & Reader Chrome → Red Letters.

### Gold · God

The Father's character, his names, his self-description, and the passages where he states plainly who he is.

> Be still, and know that I am God: I will be exalted among the heathen, I will be exalted in the earth.
> — Psalm 46:10

### Green · Life

Growth, fruit, abiding, wisdom for how to actually live. The verses that tell you what a life looks like when it is working.

> I am the vine, ye are the branches: He that abideth in me, and I in him, the same bringeth forth much fruit: for without me ye can do nothing.
> — John 15:5

### Blue · Hope

Promises, future glory, the lines you reach for on a bad Tuesday. This is the colour that fills up fastest, which is fine — that is what it is for.

> It is good that a man should both hope and quietly wait for the salvation of the LORD.
> — Lamentations 3:26

### Brown · Warning

Judgement, rebuke, temptation, the passages that make you uncomfortable. Nobody's favourite colour, and the one that most repays having a slot.

> There is a way which seemeth right unto a man, but the end thereof are the ways of death.
> — Proverbs 14:12

Brown is doing the most work in this system precisely because it is the one nobody reaches for by instinct. A page where every mark is blue and gold is a page that has been read selectively.

## The rule that keeps it useful

Highlight only what you would want to re-read, not what you agree with.

That single sentence does most of the sorting. Most highlighting decays because it records recognition — *yes, true, amen* — rather than anything you would go looking for later. A verse you would not deliberately return to does not need a colour.

The second rule: if a chapter ends up entirely highlighted, the system has failed for that chapter. Pick the one or two lines that carry it and leave the rest alone.

## Taking a highlight back off

Tap the verse and choose the same colour again — it clears, rather than adding a second colour on top.

This matters more than it sounds. A highlighting system you cannot undo is one you stop using carefully, because every mark feels permanent. Being able to remove one in two taps keeps the whole thing honest.

## Finding them again later

Highlights are only half of a system. The other half is being able to retrieve them.

Home → Highlights lists everything you have marked, and it sorts four ways:

- Added date, oldest first
- Added date, newest first (the default)
- Bible reference
- Highlight colour

The colour sort is the interesting one: it lets you pull up every brown verse in your Bible, which is effectively a personal list of everything that made you uneasy. It only works while the All colour chip is selected — filter to a single colour and the list falls back to newest first, since there is nothing left to group.

## What a highlight is not

Worth separating, because the app keeps them apart on purpose:

- A **highlight** carries no text. It is a colour, nothing else.
- A **note** carries what you actually wrote, and shows a preview on the verse row.
- A **favourite** is a marker of affection for a verse — a keep-list, not a colour system.
- A **tag** is subject matter you will want to filter by later.

If you find yourself trying to encode an idea into a colour, that idea belongs in a note. Colours sort; notes explain.

## Two minutes now

The value of a colour system is not in the moment you set it up. It is in the day, years later, when you open a familiar chapter and the page tells you, at a glance, that you have been circling the same promise for a decade.

Five colours is enough for a whole Bible, and few enough that you never have to pause and decide.

If you are starting from nothing and do not know where to open first, the verse collections at [/bible-verses](/bible-verses) group passages by what you are carrying today — a reasonable place to make your first few marks.
